Why

    Which single intervention helps most?

    Simulation coefficients are approximate, evidence-informed estimates (weight-loss/BP/lipid/ glycemic meta-analyses, structured-exercise and interrupted-sitting trials, sleep-restriction and stress-reduction studies). This is a decision-support prototype, not a validated clinical dosing tool. Fasting glucose and HbA1c are tracked and forecast here as outcomes, exactly like lipids, CRP, and liver fat — none of them are ever used as inputs to the risk model itself, since they're diagnostic criteria for diabetes, not independent risk factors.

    Projected 12-month trajectory

    Trajectories interpolate smoothly toward each scenario's projected risk over 12 months and are illustrative, not a validated longitudinal forecast. Uses the same simulated intervention set as the Simulator tab.

    Know Yourself

    General education about the numbers Metis tracks and what tends to help — not personalized medical advice.

    Intended Use

    Metis is a clinical decision-support tool, intended to help identify people who may benefit from further clinical evaluation for five metabolic conditions (Type 2 Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome, Fatty Liver Disease, Chronic Kidney Disease, Hypertension), using routinely available anthropometric, laboratory, and lifestyle data.

    Metis is not a diagnostic device. A Metis risk estimate does not confirm or rule out any condition, and is not intended to be the sole basis for any clinical decision. Every risk estimate should be interpreted by a qualified healthcare professional alongside clinical judgment, patient history, physical examination, and confirmatory diagnostic testing (e.g. fasting glucose or HbA1c for diabetes, a renal panel for kidney disease, liver imaging for fatty liver disease, as clinically appropriate).

    Metis has been validated internally, on retrospective population survey data (NHANES, August 2021–August 2023) held out from model training — see the validation reports in backend/training/ for full methodology and results. It has not yet undergone prospective clinical validation against real patient outcomes. This distinction matters: internal validation confirms the model generalizes to unseen rows from the same survey: it does not yet confirm that a person flagged as high-risk today will go on to develop that condition. Treat Metis accordingly — as a well-validated statistical screening aid, not a clinically proven one yet.

    Understanding your numbers

    BMI (Body Mass Index)
    Weight relative to height. A screening tool, not a diagnosis — it doesn't distinguish muscle from fat, so it's most useful alongside waist circumference.
    Waist-to-height ratio
    Waist circumference divided by height. Tracks visceral (abdominal) fat, which matters more for metabolic risk than weight alone. Under 0.5 is generally considered healthier.
    HDL / LDL / Triglycerides
    HDL ("good" cholesterol) helps clear fat from the bloodstream — higher is better. LDL and triglycerides circulate fat and, when elevated, are linked to cardiovascular and metabolic risk.
    CRP (C-reactive protein)
    A marker of systemic inflammation. Chronic low-grade inflammation is increasingly linked to insulin resistance, so a persistently elevated CRP is worth discussing with a doctor.
    HOMA-IR / Fasting insulin
    Estimates insulin resistance — how hard your body has to work to keep blood sugar in range. Higher values mean your cells respond less readily to insulin.
    Fasting glucose & HbA1c
    The two standard diagnostic labs for diabetes. Fasting glucose is a snapshot; HbA1c reflects average blood sugar over roughly the past 2-3 months. Metis tracks both and forecasts how interventions might shift them, but never uses either as a risk-model input — that would mean using the diagnosis to predict itself.
    Liver fat (CAP score)
    From liver elastography. Fatty liver (steatosis) is both a cause and consequence of insulin resistance, and often improves substantially with weight loss.
    PHQ-9 (stress/mood score)
    A standard, validated screening scale (0–27) for stress and low-mood symptoms — not a diagnosis. Chronic stress is associated with elevated cortisol, which can worsen insulin resistance.

    Reading your risk score

    The percentage on your Home tab is a model estimate, calibrated on real 2021–2023 NHANES survey data — it's the model's best guess given your inputs, not a diagnosis. A few things worth keeping in mind:

    • Small changes in borderline inputs (e.g. BMI right at a threshold) can shift the percentage more than they'd shift your actual health.
    • The Risk & Drivers tab shows exactly which factors pushed your score up (rose) or down (teal) — that's usually more actionable than the headline number itself.
    • The model can't see everything about you — family history, genetics, and medications aren't part of its inputs.
    • Only a clinician, usually with a fasting glucose or HbA1c test, can actually diagnose prediabetes or diabetes.

    Understanding your 5 risk scores

    Metis now screens for five related metabolic conditions, each with its own model and its own excluded predictors (a condition's own diagnostic markers are never used to predict itself).

    Type 2 Diabetes
    Predicted from independent risk factors; fasting glucose and HbA1c are tracked separately as outcomes, never as inputs, since they define the diagnosis itself.
    Metabolic Syndrome
    A cluster of 3+ of: abdominal obesity, high triglycerides, low HDL, elevated blood pressure, elevated fasting glucose. A strong predictor of future diabetes and heart disease even before any single measurement crosses a diagnostic line on its own.
    Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD)
    Fat buildup in the liver, measured here by elastography (CAP score) — not a blood test. Closely tied to insulin resistance in both directions: it's often an early sign of developing metabolic problems, and losing weight is one of the most effective ways to reverse it.
    Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
    Reduced kidney filtering capacity (eGFR, calculated from a creatinine blood test), or a prior diagnosis. Diabetes, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome are all major risk factors for CKD over time, which is part of why they're tracked together here.
    Hypertension
    High blood pressure, by direct measurement or prior diagnosis. Often has no symptoms at all, which is why it's sometimes called a "silent" risk factor — regular measurement matters more than how you feel.

    Everyday tips

    Nutrition

    Favor whole foods with fiber (vegetables, legumes, whole grains) over refined carbs and added sugar. Fiber slows glucose absorption and tends to improve lipids over time.

    Movement

    Both structured exercise and simply breaking up long sitting stretches help — a short walk after meals is a well-supported, low-effort habit.

    Sleep

    Short sleep is consistently linked to reduced insulin sensitivity. Consistent sleep/wake timing tends to matter as much as total hours.

    Stress

    Chronic stress raises cortisol, which can raise blood sugar independent of diet. Whatever reliably lowers your stress — walking, breathing exercises, social connection — has metabolic value too.

    Alcohol & smoking

    Both are independently linked to insulin resistance and liver fat. Reducing either has measurable benefits, seen in this app's own simulator.

    When to see a doctor

    Consider booking a checkup if:

    • Your predicted risk is elevated and you haven't had fasting glucose or HbA1c checked recently.
    • You notice new symptoms — unusual thirst, frequent urination, unexplained fatigue or weight change.
    • You have a family history of type 2 diabetes and haven't discussed screening with a clinician.
    • Any number here (blood pressure, CRP, liver markers) is far outside typical ranges — this tool flags patterns, a clinician interprets them.
